The Foundation Play

How Concepta stays indispensable in an AI-first world

Align on Phase 1
We decide this together. Not all 16 can go first.
Before we assign anything to Phase 1, we need to do three things as a team: agree on which capabilities carry the most immediate risk, identify who has real familiarity and experience with each one, and pressure-test the shortlist with Stargate dev to understand what AI workflows they are already building around these areas. Phase 1 is not a list someone hands down. It is what this team commits to owning.
Step 1   Prioritize
Which capabilities must happen first?

Walk the 16 as a team. Flag what is highest risk if left unaddressed -- client exposure, security liability, delivery failure. The team votes. No one person decides.

Step 2   Own It
Who has the familiarity to own it?

For each Phase 1 pick, we identify who has the hands-on experience. That person becomes the product owner -- they define the process, workflow, tooling, and standard. Not a coordinator. An owner.

Step 3   Validate
What is Stargate already building?

Take the shortlist to Stargate dev. For each capability, ask: what AI workflows are you building here? Where can we plug in versus build from scratch? Feasibility informs sequencing.

What it means to be a capability product owner
  • ›You define what "done" looks like for this capability across all engagements
  • ›You document the process, workflow, and tooling decisions
  • ›You set the standard and hold the team to it on every project
  • ›You are the escalation point when a project deviates from the standard
  • ›You work directly with Stargate dev on AI workflow integration for your area
  • ›You own the iteration -- as the capability matures, you drive it forward
The Stargate Thread

Stargate dev is actively building AI workflows. Before Phase 1 is locked, every capability owner needs to sit with Stargate dev and ask: what are you building that touches this area? Some of our Phase 1 capabilities may already have AI coverage in progress. Others may need Stargate input to determine whether a manual process is even the right answer. Feasibility conversations happen before commitments are made -- not after.

Vertical Foundations
The foundation is not one-size-fits-all. It is calibrated to the industry.
A software agency serving a bank, an e-commerce retailer, and an internal operations team is not serving the same foundation problem three times. The underlying 16 capabilities are the same -- the calibration reflects the actual risk environment each client operates in.
Financial Services
Banks, lending platforms, payment-adjacent software
  • ›PCI-DSS and SOC 2 gates built into every deployment pipeline -- not bolted on after launch
  • ›Immutable audit trails for all data access and financial transactions, structured to survive a regulatory inquiry
  • ›Incident response runbooks accounting for financial and reputational cost of downtime
  • ›Observability is not optional -- without telemetry, client claims become the narrative
E-Commerce & Consumer Products
Retail platforms, consumer apps, subscription businesses
  • ›Peak load architecture designed before the campaign -- not after the crash
  • ›Payment rails and checkout flows tested, monitored, and incident-ready at all times
  • ›Feature flag infrastructure so clients ship experiments safely without touching core logic
  • ›Audience intelligence connecting campaign activity to product behavior -- closed loop from spend to retention
Internal & Admin Systems
Ops tools, internal portals, workflow automation
  • ›SSO and RBAC implemented at the foundation level -- not hacked in later when compliance asks
  • ›Audit logging for every action by every user, by default, structured for retrieval
  • ›Documentation standards that ensure the tool survives the person who built it
  • ›Change governance that applies even when the client's own team is building -- internal tools often bypass scrutiny applied to consumer-facing software

Where This Goes
The foundation becomes a system. The system runs inside Stargate.
The MVP capabilities and playbooks are the first layer. Here is how they evolve into something that scales.
Layer 1  |  Ownership
Each MVP capability gets a named product owner
Ownership is assigned based on familiarity and experience with that specific capability area -- not availability. The owner defines what good looks like, maintains the standard, and is accountable when something slips. One name. Not a committee.
Layer 2  |  Playbooks
Each capability gets a living operational playbook
Four things defined per capability: the workflow (how it operates day to day), failure fallbacks (what happens when a tool goes down, a vendor changes, or a team member leaves), evolution triggers (what causes a review -- tool updates, new regulations, contract changes, performance gaps), and defined policies for how and when it runs. This is not a static document. It updates as the ecosystem around it changes.
Layer 3  |  Skills
Playbooks get encoded as executable AI skills
Once a playbook is defined, it becomes a skill -- a structured, repeatable workflow that AI can execute. Run a capability health check against a codebase. Generate an audit report for a client engagement. Flag when a tool update changes a workflow. Prompt an owner when a policy review is due. The playbook stops being a document and starts being something that runs.
Layer 4  |  Stargate Integration
The foundation and the AI tooling become one system
Over time, the capability skills live inside Stargate. Every workflow Stargate runs for a client automatically passes through the foundational standard -- not as a separate audit after the fact, but built into the workflow itself. Stargate delivers the speed. The capability layer is what makes that speed trustworthy. That is how this answers the question of how AI connects to the foundation.
